# Research: analysis, report, review

Three jobs in order. Analyse through sequential lenses, build the report as a narrative, then try to break it.

**Standing rules for any machine-assisted pass over research data:**

- **Every quote must be verifiable against the source.** Quote verbatim, carry the participant ID and where it sits in the transcript, and never merge two speakers into one line. Fabricated and composite quotes are the signature failure of machine-assisted qualitative work, and a report does not recover once one is found in it. A quote that cannot be located in the source does not go in.
- **Consent scope governs everything.** Participants agreed to a study, not to publication, a repository, or a model. Before analysis, establish what was consented to, which identifiers must be stripped, where the data may be stored and processed, and what may be quoted externally. Internal stakeholder sessions carry the same duty: name roles, not people, unless they have agreed otherwise.
- **Say what the machine did.** A report whose themes were produced with model assistance records that in the method section, with what the human checked.

# 1 · Analysis: sequential lenses

One pass over a transcript set produces themes. Several passes, each asking a different question, produce findings that build on one another. Run lenses **in order** — later ones draw on what earlier ones established, and the synthesis draws on everything.

Every lens has the same four parts, and the third is the one that does the work:

- **Why it matters** — the decision this lens changes. A lens that changes no decision does not get run.
- **What to look for** — specific, numbered, concrete.
- **What NOT to do** — the analytical errors this particular lens invites. Write this before the look-for list.
- **What it produces** — named deliverables, not "insights".

Adapt the set to the study, and drop lenses the study cannot support. On a small or fast piece of work, lenses 1, 2 and 7 plus the synthesis carry most of the value.

**Lens 1 · Sense-making and mental models.** How participants conceptualise and categorise the thing — not what they want, but how they think about what they get. Natural categories, information hierarchy, metaphors (spatial, journey), how they see their own role in the chain. *Do not* project your model onto their language — let their categories emerge, then note alignment. *Do not* skip participants who seem less articulate; confusion is data about cognitive gaps.

**Lens 2 · Behavioural evidence.** The say-do gap. Compensating behaviours, triage patterns, what happens before and after the moment you are studying. **Every workaround is a design specification in disguise.** *Do not* judge the gap. *Do not* assume stated preferences are unreliable — sometimes people describe aspirational behaviour they would adopt if the thing were better.

**Lens 3 · Jobs and value.** What is each thing hired to do, and what does done well look like? Functional, **emotional**, and **social** jobs ("look competent in front of the board"). Over-served and under-served jobs. *Do not* conflate the producer's intended job with the consumer's actual job — "demonstrate rigour" versus "feel confident enough to decide" is where most of the gap lives.

**Lens 4 · Design evidence.** Every concrete attribute participants associate with something working or failing, in their own words: structural, visual, navigational, tonal, length, anti-patterns, positive exemplars. *Do not* reduce specific evidence to generic themes — "participants want clarity" is not a specification; "recommendation on the first page with the decision labelled" is. *Do not* assume participants mean the same thing by the same word.

**Lens 5 · Trust, credibility and relationship.** Where trust sits (person, institution, process, format), what is delegated versus contested, trust-building and trust-breaking moments, variation by role. *Do not* treat trust as binary or fixed; it shifts by topic and stakes.

**Lens 6 · Comparative reference.** What participants have seen elsewhere that works, including outside the sector, and where the competitive floor sits. *Do not* assume their descriptions of competitors are accurate — perception drives satisfaction anyway. *Do not* limit to the sector; the most transferable principles usually come from unrelated contexts.

**Pass A · Structured activity data.** Where co-design or card-sort output exists, treat it as pattern extraction rather than interpretation: ordering, exclusions, convergence strength, combinations, cross-scenario comparison. Exclusion is as informative as inclusion. *Do not* average out differences — report both sides of a split.

**Pass B · Ecosystem and channel.** The whole experience across a cycle: touchpoint hierarchy by stated importance *and* by behaviour, gaps, excess, continuity, onward sharing. *Do not* produce a flat list — map the relationships. *Do not* assume the current state is a baseline to tweak; it may need restructuring.

**Lens 7 · Strategic risk and organisational readiness.** Run across all previous outputs. Cost of inaction, behaviour change required, capability gaps, resistance points, quick wins versus investment versus transformation, and assumptions the evidence contradicts. *Do not* be diplomatic at the expense of clarity. *Do not* treat all recommendations as equal priority — force-rank them.

**Synthesis.** Runs last, across everything. **Do not summarise. Synthesise.** It must contain: the headline story in three or four sentences; **convergence across lenses, with the caveat that decides whether it means anything** — agreement between methods is strong evidence only where the methods have independent sampling frames, because two methods run over the same recruited group agree about that group rather than about the population, and convergence then confirms the frame instead of the finding (failure mode 1); **tensions**, where lenses contradict each other or where what people want is impractical; **uncomfortable truths** most likely to challenge stakeholder assumptions; audience divergence; prioritised recommendations in three tiers (do now within existing processes / invest / transform); and a **confidence map** separating well-evidenced findings from emerging signals, with sample limitations stated plainly.

# 2 · The report

## The two-artefact rule

1. **The long version, for nobody.** Answers every question the work could reasonably be asked, in full, with evidence attached. It is what makes the author unbeatable when questioned.
2. **The presented version, cut out of the first.** Never written separately — always reduced, so every claim in the room has a fuller answer behind it.

If only one document exists, it is too thin to defend and too dense to follow.

## The arc

Findings presented as a list read as a list of complaints. The same findings in an arc read as an explanation, and explanations are what people act on.

1. **The world** — why people behave like this, *before* any finding. Habit, status quo bias, cognitive load, organisational incentives. Without a world, every finding sounds like users being difficult.
2. **The characters** — the behavioural groups, in prose, as people with goals, mindset and emotional response. Not a table of attributes.
3. **The day** — one journey through their eyes, present tense. Where the reader stops evaluating and starts recognising.
4. **The break** — where it fails, what it costs, who pays. End on the consequence, not the defect.
5. **The turn** — one explicit pivot from problem to possibility before any recommendation. Recommendations arriving without a turn read as a punch list.
6. **The coda** — the researcher's note. Direct address, what the author actually thinks.

**The arc must carry the contradiction.** A story with no one in it who disagrees is propaganda — and it is failure mode 7 below. The participant who broke the pattern belongs *in* the narrative, as the character who does not fit. That is what makes the rest credible.

## Narrative craft

- **Present tense for journeys, past tense for findings.** The tense shift tells the reader they have moved from evidence into experience and back.
- **Invite the reader in once.** "Let's step into their world for a moment." Once per report; twice is a gimmick.
- **Name the groups so people repeat the names.** "The Doers and the Trackers" travels through an organisation; "Segment A" does not. The name is the finding's delivery mechanism.
- **Consequence beats, sparingly.** A short question, then the answer: *The result? The tool is experienced as a burden rather than a solution.*
- **Borrow the organisation's own vocabulary for its own rituals.** If they call the pitch process "the beauty parade", write about the beauty parade. It makes the report theirs rather than yours.
- **Collect storylines, not attributes.** Ask for the moment something changed and every key event after it. A journey a participant narrated beats one you assembled.
- **One quote per beat**, attributed to a participant ID, chosen because it carries the point rather than because it is quotable — and checked against the transcript before it ships.
- **Open with the anthropological note where behaviour is the story.** Explaining *why* people resist before showing *that* they resist converts a complaint into a mechanism, and mechanisms are designable.

## Structure

Cover · **research overview** (the research question in one sentence, not a list of objectives; method, dates, duration, location, recruitment and composition) · **TL;DR** · **contextual note** *(the world)* · **the users** *(characters)* · **their journey** *(the day)* · **themes** with verbatim evidence *(the break)* · **comparison matrix** where relevant, since a table survives a steering meeting in a way a themes list does not · **bridging the gap** *(the turn)* · **insights, needs and features**, needs stated as need + why · **recommendations**, each with the reason and the cost of not doing it · **researcher's note** *(the coda)*.

## Evidence conventions

Narrative carries the evidence; it never substitutes for it.

- **Behavioural groups, not demographic personas.**
- **Numbers, not adjectives.** "14 participants, 5 with a physical or mental disability, 3 with basic digital skills" — never "a range of users".
- **Restore the denominator without implying prevalence.** "Three of eleven, all in the same role" is honest; a percentage from a purposive sample of eleven is not. Small qualitative samples support existence and mechanism claims, not rates.
- **Attach evidence to every headline:** a quote with a participant ID, an observed behaviour, or a number.
- **Report absences.** What was looked for and not found is often more useful than what was.
- **Reconcile behavioural data with self-report**, or name the gap as a finding.
- **Recommendations trace to findings**, not to general good practice.
- **It must work without the researcher in the room.**

# 3 · Review: sixteen failure modes

Hygiene checks catch sloppiness. These catch the errors that **survive competent work**. Assess each as ruled out / unsure / exposed, and treat "unsure" as exposed: in the room the two sound identical.

**Establish two things before running it.**

**What the artefact is.** Working notes, a draft and a final report fail these differently, and family V will fire on any set of raw notes simply for being raw. Name the stage and the intended audience, and judge against what the thing has to be by the time someone acts on it. A mode that fires because the work is unfinished is not a finding.

**Which modes carry information.** The fix line tells you. Modes whose fix begins *name one*, *find one*, *identify one*, or *what evidence would have* force a return to the material and produce things a reviewer could not have written from outside it. The rest are checks answered from memory, and are often decided by the study's genre before the document is opened — an interview-only study fails 4 and 5 by construction. Run both kinds. Only the first kind should surprise you, and a flag from the second carries much less weight.

Fix upward through families I to III: the earlier the family, the more of the report inherits the error. Families IV and V are largely order-independent, and a mode there can be as consequential as anything above it.

Several modes have established names in survey methodology and psychology. Use both: the coined name is the mnemonic, the established one is what stops a methods-literate reader dismissing the set as invented vocabulary.

**I · Wrong about who you learned from.** Invalidates everything downstream; nothing in the analysis can reveal it.

1. **The Survivor Sample** — were participants selected by the very process being studied? Screeners built from the product's own users, CRM or panel make the churned, bounced and never-arrived structurally unreachable. The survey term is **coverage error**: the frame excludes part of the population, so there is no non-response to reason about. It applies equally to any dataset analysed rather than collected — a CRM, an analytics export or a service's own records hold only the people who got far enough to leave a trace. And it applies one level up: a study of a service that talks only to the people who build it has the same shape. *Fix (search):* name the population the sampling frame cannot reach and what it costs; better, recruit people who solved the problem without the product.
2. **The Articulate Majority** — would someone who cannot explain their own behaviour have survived the screener and the session? Methods select for verbal fluency, comfort being observed and 2pm availability. This is **self-selection** operating at the point of consent rather than at recruitment. *Fix (search):* find where the thinnest sessions cluster. If they cluster in one group that is a method result, and silence belongs in the report as non-response rather than recoded as a disposition.
3. **The Wrong Unit** — is the behaviour a property of the person or of the system they sit inside? Social psychology calls the underlying habit the **fundamental attribution error**: over-explaining behaviour by disposition and under-explaining it by situation. *Fix (search):* ask whether the implied fix is available to the individual interviewed. If it is not, re-attribute the finding to whoever can act on it.

**II · Wrong about what you actually measured.** The instrument leaves a fingerprint that gets reported as data.

4. **The Method's Shadow** — does the finding have the same shape as the method? Card sorts produce taxonomies, interviews narratives, diaries routines. *Fix (search):* name one finding the method could not have produced. If there isn't one, add a method or lower the confidence.
5. **The Rehearsed Answer** — what they do, or what they like to think they do? Stated preference diverges from behaviour most sharply where effort, money, status or habit are at stake, and **social desirability** does the rest. *Fix (search):* find one load-bearing claim resting on something observed or logged; where the two disagree, that is the finding.
6. **The Prototype Effect** — measuring the design, or novelty plus being watched? Reactivity and novelty both inflate first-encounter results. (The Hawthorne effect is the usual reference; the original illumination data does not actually support it, so name reactivity instead.) *Fix (check):* separate first-encounter findings from durable ones. **Declare not applicable where there is no prototype, task or observation** — stretched onto interview or workshop material this mode produces a finding-shaped statement rather than a finding.

**III · Wrong about what it means.** The data was fine; synthesis was not.

7. **The Smooth Report** — which contradictions survived compression, and which did not? Asking only "is there a contradiction?" produces a false pass, because one preserved tension is not evidence the others were kept. *Fix (search):* read the raw material against the summary layer and list every dissent that failed to make the crossing. Put the strongest back in, in the narrative, as the character who does not fit.
8. **The Borrowed Frame** — whose language are the themes in: the participants', the product team's, or last quarter's roadmap? *Fix (search):* find one theme with no existing home in the organisation's vocabulary. A participant coining their own term because the organisation's word was wrong is the strongest instance and the easiest to flatten back.
9. **The Vanishing Denominator** — how many people is "users"? *Fix (check):* restore the number at every headline, and check whether the same statement has been banked twice under different headings, which inflates apparent convergence.

**IV · True but unfalsifiable.** Nothing here is wrong; the problem is that nothing could have been.

10. **The Missing Decision Rule** — before fieldwork, could you write "if we find X we do A; if Y, B"? *Fix (search):* name the live decision this work feeds and check it is one of the questions being asked. Agree the rule with the decision-maker before recruitment; refusal to commit is itself more useful than the study.
11. **The Unfalsifiable Finding** — what specific evidence would have produced the opposite conclusion? Applies hardest to findings that feel obviously true. *Fix (search):* go and find the counter-cases in your own material. Where they exist and have been annotated as supporting the hypothesis, that annotation is the finding.
12. **The Quarter-Old Truth** — about people, or about people using this build this quarter? *Fix (check):* tag shelf life — behavioural, market, or build.

**V · True but inert.** The most common failure and the least often named. Check the maturity gate first.

13. **The Foregone Conclusion** — would this recommendation have appeared if the findings had been the opposite? *Fix (search):* identify one you would not have made three weeks ago; if there isn't one, say so in the report.
14. **The Unowned Finding** — name the person whose decision changes, not the team. *Fix (check):* a name and a decision date against each recommendation.
15. **The Absent Cost** — what happens if nobody acts? *Fix (check):* state it in the unit that room uses — churn, calls, complaints, rework, regulatory exposure.
16. **The Researcher-Dependent Report** — does it work when the author is on holiday? *Fix (check):* expand every acronym, include the artefact being discussed, and give it to someone outside the project to ask what they would do next.

When reviewing, report the exposure profile by family, keep the search findings separate from the checklist flags, and say what to go and get — not a score. The modes that cannot be ruled out are the report's real findings.
